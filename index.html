Dossier No. 323 · Unsolved case

Where does
Alexander rest?

The body of the man who conquered the world was for five hundred years the chief relic of Alexandria. It was seen by Caesar, Augustus, and Caracalla. And then it vanished — and for 2348 years no one has been able to say where the king lies.

SomaΣῶμα "body" · Σῆμα "tomb"

The mausoleum of Alexander in the center of Alexandria, built by Ptolemy IV. A walled complex where the Ptolemies themselves lay around the king. Ancient authors call it by both words.

BruchionΒρουχεῖον

The royal quarter of Alexandria — palaces, the Mouseion, the Library, and the Soma occupied up to a third of the city. Burned under Aurelian in 272, partly submerged beneath the Eastern Harbor.

DiadochiΔιάδοχοι "successors"

The generals who divided the empire. To them the body of Alexander was political capital: whoever possessed the king was the legitimate heir. That is why a real war was fought over the tomb.

Catafalqueἁρμάμαξα

A golden temple on wheels, built for two years in Babylon. Its route and theft are the opening of the whole story.

323 BC
Location of the bodyBabylon
323 BC · Babylon

Death in the palace of Nebuchadnezzar

After ten days of fever Alexander died without naming an undisputed heir. While the generals divided power, the body lay almost a week unattended in the summer heat — and, according to Curtius Rufus, showed no signs of decay. Egyptian and Chaldean embalmers prepared it for the long road; by one version, the body was placed in honey.

Curtius Rufus X, 10 · Arrian VII, 25–28 · Plutarch, "Alexander", 76–77
323–321 BC · Babylon

A golden temple on wheels

For almost two years the engineer Arrhidaeus built the catafalque: an Ionic portico of gold, a vaulted roof of golden scales set with gems, golden bells and painted panels — Alexander in a chariot, war elephants, a fleet. The cart was drawn by 64 mules in gilded crowns, and ahead of them walked diggers, leveling the road.

Diodorus Siculus XVIII, 26–28
321 BC · Syria

The theft of the century

The regent Perdiccas ordered the body taken to Aegae, the burial place of the Macedonian kings — while Alexander himself, by tradition, had asked to be buried at the oracle of Amun in Siwa. But in Syria the cortege was met by Ptolemy with an army. He won over the escort and led the king away to Egypt: possession of the founder's body gave the dynasty legitimacy. Perdiccas set off in pursuit, but died by the Nile at the hands of his own officers.

Diodorus XVIII, 28 · Pausanias I, 6 · Arrian, "Events after Alexander"
321 — c. 280 BC · Memphis

Forty years in the ancient capital

Ptolemy buried Alexander in Memphis "according to Macedonian rite" — probably in the Saqqara necropolis, near the Serapeum. Andrew Chugg believes the coffin was the unused sarcophagus of the last pharaoh, Nectanebo II. Here, too, the former bodyguard wrote his memoirs of the campaigns — the main source for Arrian.

Pausanias I, 6–7 · Curtius Rufus X, 10
c. 280 BC · Alexandria

Moving to the city of Alexander

Ptolemy II Philadelphus transferred the body to the new capital that the king himself had founded. The first tomb probably stood in the royal quarter of the Bruchion. Alexander became the divine patron of the city and the dynasty: documents across Egypt were dated by the name of his priest.

Pausanias I, 7 · Strabo XVII, 1, 8
c. 215 BC · Alexandria

Soma: the dynastic mausoleum

Ptolemy IV Philopator built the Soma in the center of the city — a walled complex where the tombs of the Ptolemies were arranged around Alexander's tomb. According to Strabo, it was "part of the royal palaces." The Street of the Soma crossed the Canopic Way — the main avenue of the city, and that crossroads is still being sought.

Zenobius III, 94 · Strabo XVII, 1, 8 · Achilles Tatius V, 1
89 BC · Alexandria

Gold into coins

Ptolemy X Alexander I, in debt to his mercenaries, melted down the golden sarcophagus into coin and replaced it with a glass — crystal — one. The Alexandrians drove out the sacrilegious king, but the transparent coffin remained: from then on, every visitor could see Alexander himself.

Strabo XVII, 1, 8
48–30 BC · Alexandria

Caesar and Augustus at the tomb

Julius Caesar descended into the tomb in 48 BC while pursuing Pompey. Octavian in 30 BC, having conquered Egypt, ordered the sarcophagus brought out, placed a golden crown on it, strewed the body with flowers — and, according to Cassius Dio, touching the face, broke off a piece of the nose. He refused to look at the tombs of the Ptolemies: "I wanted to see a king, not corpses."

Lucan X · Suetonius, "Augustus", 18 · Cassius Dio LI, 16
40–215 AD · Alexandria

The last pilgrims

Caligula took Alexander's breastplate from the tomb and wore it in public. Septimius Severus in 200 AD sealed the crypt, hiding secret books inside. In 215 AD Caracalla, who considered himself a new Alexander, took off his purple cloak, rings, and belt and laid them on the tomb. This is the last reliable testimony about the body.

Suetonius, "Caligula", 52 · Cassius Dio LXXVI, 13 · Herodian IV, 8
272–400 AD · Alexandria

Disappearance

Under Aurelian in 272 the Bruchion burned. In 365 the city was struck by a tsunami. In 391, by the edicts of Theodosius, Christians destroyed the Serapeum. Around 390 Libanius still mentions the body of Alexander on display, and around 400 John Chrysostom already asks: "Where is the tomb of Alexander?" The trail breaks off.

Ammianus Marcellinus XXII, 16 · Libanius, Oration XLIX · John Chrysostom, Homily XXVI

VI

Chronicle of searches

For fifteen hundred years people have searched for the tomb: medieval pilgrims, Napoleon's scholars, Schliemann, an obsessed waiter, and modern archaeologists with ground-penetrating radar.

VII

Why it was not found

The most famous mausoleum of antiquity could not simply dissolve. But Alexandria did almost everything to make it happen.

Reason 01

Earthquakes and subsidence

Alexandria stands on a subsiding coast: over two millennia the shoreline has dropped 6–8 meters. The earthquakes of 365, 956, 1303, and 1323 brought down the Pharos — and probably everything still standing in the royal quarter.

Reason 02

The tsunami of July 21, 365

Ammianus Marcellinus describes how the sea retreated and then a wave threw ships onto the roofs of houses. Thousands of Alexandrians died; the coastal Bruchion was devastated. The anniversary of the catastrophe was still observed in the city a century later.

Reason 03

Wars and a change of faith

In 272 Aurelian burned the royal quarter while suppressing a revolt. In 391 Christians destroyed the Serapeum. The tomb of a deified pagan in the center of the capital of Egyptian Christianity was doomed — either destroyed or hidden.

Reason 04

A city upon a city

Ancient layers lie 8–12 meters beneath modern streets, below the water table. Above them is the dense development of a city of five million. One can dig only on rare vacant lots and in courtyards.

Reason 05

The stone went into the walls

Marble and granite from ancient buildings were for centuries burned into lime and relaid in fortresses and mosques. Fort Qaitbay is built of blocks from the Pharos lighthouse. The Soma was most likely dismantled in the same way — stone by stone.

Reason 06

Oblivion

The medieval city shrank to the isthmus by the harbor, and the old center became a wasteland. By the time Napoleon arrived in 1798, some eight thousand people lived in Alexandria — and no one any longer remembered where the palaces of the Ptolemies had stood.
